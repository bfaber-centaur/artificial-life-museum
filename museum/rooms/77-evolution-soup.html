Evolution in a soup

In the conserved-matter room next door, matter flows and is never made or destroyed. Here every cell also carries a small genome, ten numbers that set how it responds to its neighbours, and the genome rides along with the matter. Now and then a patch of genome mutates. Bodies can only grow by taking matter from other bodies. We seeded worlds with sixteen founder species and let them run.

One physics, many body plans

What you see. Within one world, founders that differ only in their genomes build different bodies: in the top-left world, worms next to rings next to dots. Most worlds then go quiet: the mosaic settles and sits. One kernel set (16, bottom row) never settled, and that is where the rest of this room happens.

The amoeba and the plankton

What you see. On the left a slime grows into a giant amoeba and engulfs the colony of dot-shaped plankton; whatever it swallows takes its genome. On the right the plankton survive, and the blobs become mottled mosaics of many genomes.

What the ledger says. In four starting worlds out of four, blending ends with one body holding 68–89% of all matter in bodies; particulate heredity keeps 70–150 bodies, the largest holding at most 47% {{claim:C077}}.

Darwin's nightmare, in a petri dish

What you see. In the left fan every mutation leaves a bright wedge that tapers back into the crowd as its matter mixes with its neighbours'. On the right the stripes stay sharp.

What the ledger says. Genome diversity falls under blending and rises under particulate heredity in all four paired worlds {{claim:C076}}. In a world of one species, a single mutant patch dissolves under blending but persists when genomes are passed on whole {{claim:C078}}.

Why it matters. In 1867 the engineer Fleeming Jenkin raised this exact objection to Darwin: if offspring blend their parents, a new variant is halved at every mating and soon vanishes, so selection has nothing to work with. The answer was Mendel's: inheritance comes in particles that do not mix. The soup asks the same question of physics and gets the same answer. The particulate world keeps more variety, but we do not claim that its extra variety is adaptive: some of it is mutations piling up because nothing averages them away.

Fit, or lucky?

Under whole-genome inheritance four of the twelve mutants above grew. Did they win because of what they are, or because of where they landed? We took each of the twelve mutant genomes and dropped it into the same world again, at six new places each: 72 more runs.

What you see. Mutant 8 lost half its matter the first time and grew in all six replays. Mutant 4 nearly doubled the first time and shrank in five of six.

What the ledger says. The first trial does not predict the replays: the four biggest first-trial growers grew in 10 of 24 replays, the other genomes in 27 of 48 {{claim:C099}}. Genomes do differ: four grew in five or six places out of six, one shrank in five, and shuffling the labels rarely spreads them that far {{claim:C100}}. But one genome's outcomes at different places vary about twice as much as the genomes differ from each other {{claim:C101}}.

Why it matters. Population geneticists have said this since the 1920s: a new mutation, even a good one, is usually lost to bad luck, and a winner is often just a lucky one. Fitness tilts the odds; it does not decide the single case. To call a creature fitter you have to rerun it.

Luck has a baseline

Population geneticists never judge a mutant on its own: they compare it with a neutral marker, a patch that differs from its neighbours by nothing that matters, dropped in the same place. Whatever happens to the marker is luck. We went back to the 72 places of the replays above and dropped a neutral marker at each one: the resident genome nudged by one part in a million, so we can follow where its matter goes.

What you see. Points hug the diagonal: where the marker did well, the mutant did well. On the right, mutant 4, which looked like a loser (it grew in only one replay), beats the marker at all six of its places; it was dealt bad places. Mutant 8, which grew everywhere, barely beats the marker: it was dealt good ones.

What the ledger says. Neutral markers grew at 31 of 72 places and were lost at 10, against 37 and 6 for the mutants {{claim:C116}}. A mutant's fate and the marker's fate at the same place are strongly correlated {{claim:C117}}. Against the marker, mutants come out ahead at 45 of 72 places, but the differences between genomes are no longer clearly significant {{claim:C118}}.

Why it matters. This is the control the earlier replays were missing, and it changes the story: judged alone, mutant 4 looked unfit and mutant 8 fit; judged against luck at the same places, the order flips. Experimental evolutionists compete every strain against a marked reference for exactly this reason. We have not found a Lenia or Flow-Lenia study that does it (see the {{page:so-what-evolution|So what? wing}}).
