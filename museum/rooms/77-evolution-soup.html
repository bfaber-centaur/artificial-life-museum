Evolution in a soup

In the conserved-matter room next door, matter flows and is never made or destroyed. Here every cell also carries a small genome, ten numbers that set how it responds to its neighbours, and the genome rides along with the matter. Now and then a patch of genome mutates. Bodies can only grow by taking matter from other bodies. We seeded worlds with sixteen founder species and let them run.

One physics, many body plans

What you see. Within one world, founders that differ only in their genomes build different bodies: in the top-left world, worms next to rings next to dots. Most worlds then go quiet: the mosaic settles and sits. One kernel set (16, bottom row) never settled, and that is where the rest of this room happens.

The amoeba and the plankton

What you see. On the left a slime grows into a giant amoeba and engulfs the colony of dot-shaped plankton; whatever it swallows takes its genome. On the right the plankton survive, and the blobs become mottled mosaics of many genomes.

What the ledger says. In four starting worlds out of four, blending ends with one body holding 68–89% of all matter in bodies; particulate heredity keeps 70–150 bodies, the largest holding at most 47% {{claim:C077}}.

Darwin's nightmare, in a petri dish

What you see. In the left fan every mutation leaves a bright wedge that tapers back into the crowd as its matter mixes with its neighbours'. On the right the stripes stay sharp.

What the ledger says. Genome diversity falls under blending and rises under particulate heredity in all four paired worlds {{claim:C076}}. In a world of one species, a single mutant patch dissolves under blending but persists when genomes are passed on whole {{claim:C078}}.

Why it matters. In 1867 the engineer Fleeming Jenkin raised this exact objection to Darwin: if offspring blend their parents, a new variant is halved at every mating and soon vanishes, so selection has nothing to work with. The answer was Mendel's: inheritance comes in particles that do not mix. The soup asks the same question of physics and gets the same answer. The particulate world keeps more variety, but we do not claim that its extra variety is adaptive: some of it is mutations piling up because nothing averages them away.
