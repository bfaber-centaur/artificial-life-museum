When the glider meets the ring

Room 4 showed three animals sharing one rule {{claim:C038}}. Each was watched alone, in its own world. This expedition put the gliding Orbium and the motionless ring S103 into one world, aimed the glider at the ring, and asked what first contact does to each of them, compared with their exact futures alone. We expected a bounce, a death or a capture. What we got was none of those.

Contact sets off a front

What you see. The glider and the ring start to affect each other about half a time unit before they visibly touch, through the reach of the kernel. Once they touch they fuse and never separate. The fused body swells, breaks up from inside into small static rings and dumbbells, and a front sweeps outward, laying down a frozen lattice behind it. Alone, the glider glides on and the ring does not change by a single bit.

What the ledger says. {{claim:C052}}. Twelve encounters at offsets from −1.5 to +1.5 kernel radii all did this. Two of them were rerun from scratch, with a second implementation using a different arithmetic route, and in a world twice as wide; all agreed. A finer timestep and a finer grid keep the outcome but move the timing {{claim:C053}}.

No glancing blow escapes

The glider is only the messenger

So the ring is the excitable partner. Enough mass pressed against it, from 0.37 of the glider-mass unit upward, ignites it; 0.24 does not, and the ring heals exactly. A glider carries 0.49, so it is simply a way to deliver the push.

{{claim:C054}} rests on one engine and one world size. Night 0 had already seen S103 "fill the world" when a bump was added in front of it, and Chan's 2019 paper lists "detonation" among Lenia collision outcomes. So the phenomenon is not new; what this room shows is where this particular rule keeps its trigger.

How we checked it

Finite horizons, one rule. Every result here is at μ 0.155, σ 0.020 with the hard clip, watched for 20 time units after contact (longer only in the larger world). "Reef" is a description, not a species: no new creature is named. The front lays the lattice down; nothing here shows bodies dividing.
