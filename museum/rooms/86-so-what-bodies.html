How bodies build themselves

No cell in an embryo knows the shape of the whole animal. Each one reacts to its neighbours, and a body comes out. That is the same bargain Lenia makes: local rules, global form. Here the bargain has paid off in real biology and engineering.

One chemistry, many patterns

What you see. Top left, the chemical covers everything in a smooth sheet. Bottom right, it dies out. In between runs a narrow diagonal band where all the interesting things live: labyrinths, stripes, spot lattices, a few lonely spots, and worlds that are still churning after 10,000 steps.

What the ledger says. 40 of the 100 worlds end as a uniform sheet and 28 empty; the other 32 hold spots, labyrinths or moving structure, all in the band between {{claim:C113}}. Pearson mapped this in 1993; we redrew it to show how small a change in two rates turns one pattern into another.

Evidence Turing was right about fingers and fish

In 1952 Alan Turing showed on paper that two chemicals that react and spread at different speeds can turn a uniform tissue into spots or stripes, with no blueprint. For decades it was a beautiful idea with little proof. Then the evidence came. Kondo & Asai (1995) watched the stripes of the angelfish Pomacanthus split and rearrange as the fish grew, as a reaction-diffusion model predicts. Sheth and colleagues (2012) reduced the dose of Hox genes in mice and got more, thinner fingers: the wavelength changed, just as turning a knob in the atlas above changes the pattern. Raspopovic and colleagues (2014) identified a three-molecule Turing network (Bmp, Sox9, Wnt) that patterns digits.

that every biological pattern is a Turing pattern. Many are not, and the real networks have more molecules than Gray-Scott has.

A body plan stored as voltage

Evidence Two-headed worms that stay two-headed

Cut a planarian flatworm into pieces and each piece grows back a whole worm. Durant, Levin and colleagues (2017) briefly disturbed the electrical voltage pattern across the cells of regenerating fragments. Some regrew with two heads. More surprising: the ones that looked normal were not. Cut again later, in plain water, they produced two-headed worms in the same ratio. The new body plan was stored not in the genes, which were unchanged, but in a pattern of cell voltages, and it could be switched back. Levin's program treats these voltage patterns as instructions that can be edited, with an eye to regeneration and cancer (Levin 2021).

Our link is an Analogy. A Lenia creature is also a pattern that remembers its shape after damage because the shape is a stable state of the rule, not a stored drawing. Our {{page:edges|damage room}} measures how much it can take. Whether voltage patterns in worms behave like attractors in Lenia is a hypothesis, not something our rooms show.

Durant et al. 2017; Levin 2021 (review).

Cellular automata that learn to grow

Tool Growing neural cellular automata

Lenia's rule was found by hand. Mordvintsev, Randazzo, Niklasson and Levin (2020) let a neural network learn the local rule instead: every cell runs the same tiny network, sees only its neighbours, and is trained until a single seed grows into a target image (a lizard emoji). Trained to keep the pattern stable, the rules often learned to regenerate it after damage, and could be trained to regenerate it outright. It is a working model of how local rules could encode a whole body, and a tool for studying regeneration and robustness in other systems.

how real embryos compute. The network is trained by gradient descent, which no embryo does.

Living machines

Tool Xenobots: designed by evolution in a computer, built from frog cells

Kriegman, Blackiston, Levin and Bongard (2020) used an evolutionary algorithm to design body shapes in simulation, then built the best designs by hand from frog skin and heart cells. The living results moved roughly as predicted. In 2021 the same team found that such cell clusters, put among loose cells, push them into piles that become new clusters: “kinematic self-replication”, a way of reproducing not known in any organism. A design found by AI kept the replication going for more generations.

Our link: our {{page:replicators|replicator room}} has Lenia creatures that copy themselves by dividing {{claim:C074}}, and genomes that compete {{claim:C092}}. Xenobots are real cells and our replicators are numbers, so the link is Analogy. What they share is the lesson that copying can arise from shape and movement alone.

Kriegman et al. 2020; Kriegman et al. 2021.

Tool A thousand Kilobots

Rubenstein, Cornejo and Nagpal (2014) built a swarm of about a thousand small, cheap robots that cooperate only through local interactions with their neighbours, and programmed them to assemble into complex two-dimensional shapes. No robot sees the shape. The hard part was an algorithm robust to the variability and errors of a thousand unreliable machines. This is artificial-life thinking as engineering: design the local rule and let the crowd build the form.

Rubenstein, Cornejo & Nagpal 2014.
