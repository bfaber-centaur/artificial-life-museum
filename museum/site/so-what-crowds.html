The “So what?” wing · Room 3 of 3

Crowds, contagion and machines that invent

Artificial life's habit is to write down simple local rules for many individuals and watch what the crowd does. That habit became a working method in epidemiology and ecology, where it is used to make real decisions. Its central question, how a system can keep producing new things forever, has become a research programme in AI.

The same disease in three worlds

What you see. Well mixed, the disease sweeps everyone in a few dozen steps. Among neighbours only, it crawls outward as a ring and takes hundreds of steps. With one far contact in a hundred, sparks land everywhere and the slow ring is gone.

What the ledger says. In the film's setting nearly everyone is infected in all three worlds, but the peak is 53–54% of people sick at once when mixed, 20–21% with 1% far contacts and 3–4% among neighbours only; the peak comes about 4 times later with far contacts and 15 to 20 times later among neighbours C114observed. For most runs to take off, the transmission chance has to be about three times higher among neighbours (0.10) than in a mixed crowd (0.03); 1% far contacts bring it down to 0.08 C115observed.

Tool Agent-based models in epidemiology

The classic epidemic equations (Kermack & McKendrick 1927) assume everyone meets everyone at random, the left panel. Keeling (1999) showed how the local structure of who-meets-whom changes whether a disease can invade at all, our right-hand chart. Watts & Strogatz (1998) showed that a few long-range links make a local network “small”, and that infectious diseases spread more easily on such networks than on regular lattices: our third world. Detailed individual-based simulations are used to plan for real outbreaks. Ferguson and colleagues (2005) simulated an emerging influenza in Southeast Asia person by person and found that targeted antivirals plus social distancing could stop it early if its basic reproduction number was below about 1.8.

The so-what: the same pathogen can flood hospitals or trickle through, depending only on the contact pattern. Flattening a curve is about who meets whom, and a handful of long trips can undo the protection that local structure gives.

anything about a real disease. Our worlds are textbook toys with made-up numbers, and the published models were built and fitted with real data. Our demo reproduces known results; it discovers nothing.

Kermack & McKendrick 1927; Keeling 1999; Watts & Strogatz 1998; Ferguson et al. 2005.

Ecology, one animal at a time

Tool Individual-based ecology

Ecologists simulate populations animal by animal when the details of space, age and behaviour matter, for example to manage a fishery or a threatened species. Grimm and colleagues (2005) set out how to keep such models honest: tune and test them against several patterns seen in the field at once, not one. That is the same discipline our ledger tries to impose on Lenia: a claim has to survive more than one check.

Grimm et al. 2005.

Evidence Spatial games: how cooperation survives

Nowak & May (1992) put the prisoner's dilemma on a grid: each cell either cooperates or defects, plays its neighbours and copies the most successful neighbour. In a well-mixed crowd defectors win; on the grid, cooperators and defectors coexist indefinitely in ever-changing patterns. It is a proof of principle that space alone can protect cooperation, which is why it counts as evidence about game dynamics, though not about any particular species.

Our link is an Analogy: in the collision zoo, what happens when two Orbium meet depends on exactly how they meet C080observed. Space and contact geometry decide outcomes in both worlds.

Nowak & May 1992.

Machines that keep inventing

Tool Open-endedness in AI

Evolution never stops producing new things. Most AI optimises a fixed goal and stops when it gets there. Artificial life has long asked what a system needs to keep inventing, and that question has moved into AI research. Novelty search (Lehman & Stanley 2011) rewards behaviour that is new instead of behaviour that scores well, and can beat direct optimisation on deceptive problems. POET (Wang, Lehman, Clune & Stanley 2019) invents new obstacle courses while it trains walkers to solve them, and transfers walkers between courses; some courses it solves cannot be solved by training on them directly. A 2024 position paper from Google DeepMind (Hughes et al.) argues that open-endedness is essential for any future artificial superhuman intelligence.

that these systems are open-ended in the way evolution is. The DeepMind paper is an argument, not a result.

Lehman & Stanley 2011; Wang et al. 2019; Hughes et al. 2024.

Tool AI that searches artificial life: ASAL

Kumar and colleagues (2024; the team includes Sakana AI and MIT) turned this around and used AI to search artificial life. Their system, ASAL, uses a vision-language foundation model to judge simulations by eye: find a world that looks like a target description, find worlds that keep producing novelty, and map the whole space of possible worlds. It works across Boids, Particle Life, Game of Life, Lenia and neural cellular automata, and reports previously unseen Lenia and Boids lifeforms.

Our link: our species hunt searched Lenia by hand and with simple scores and found no new genus: every body was a catalogued one, and catalogued bodies kept their way of moving even across kernel families C063observed. Automated search with a model that “sees” is a different instrument, and testing its finds the way our ledger tests ours would be a natural next exhibit.

Kumar et al. 2024 (preprint).

What artificial life cannot tell you

Read this before quoting any room in this wing A simulation shows what can happen under its rules. It cannot show what does happen in a cell, a patient or a city until it is tested against measurements from that cell, patient or city. Where an example here is labelled evidence, it is either evolution really happening in a computer (and so evidence about evolution in general), or a model that was checked against the real thing. Everything our own Lenia rooms show about biology is analogy. That is still worth something: analogies are where questions come from. But the answers have to come from somewhere else.
